The Fields I think need a distinguishing line

You asked for around twenty. Working through it against the revised sixty seven, I make it twenty nine, and they fall into eight neighbourhoods. I have grouped them rather than listed them flat, because a line for Social Care cannot be written without knowing it has to contrast with Social Work. The grouping is the useful part.

Health, policy and the public sector: Public Health, Health Policy, Health Administration & Management, Public Policy, Public Administration.

Money, risk and retirement: Actuarial Science, Insurance, Pensions & Retirement Benefits, Finance, Financial Planning, Investment Management.

Data, computing and technology: Statistics, Biostatistics, Bioinformatics, Computer Science, Artificial Intelligence, Technology.

Ageing and the life sciences: Biology, Aging Biology, Gerontology.

Care and social professions: Social Care, Social Work.

Food and nutrition: Nutrition Science, Dietetics.

Organisations and people: Business & Management, Organizational Behavior, Human Resources.

The built environment: Architecture & Urban Design, Housing.

If you want a shorter list, the first two groups are where the risk is concentrated. Eleven of the twenty nine sit there, and they are the ones where an accomplished professional could genuinely choose wrongly rather than merely hesitate. Technology is the single vaguest entry in the whole Register and I would give it a line whatever else you decide.

One thing worth saying about the lines themselves. Their job is not to define the Field. It is to answer one question: is this one mine. So Social Care describing the sector and the services, Social Work describing the profession and its practitioners. A sentence each, no more.

The Alias Register

This is very good, and two sections of it are better than good.

Section seven, on ambiguous abbreviations, is the part most people get wrong. Deciding that PT, OD, IR, ER and the rest should not become universal aliases because the ambiguity outweighs the benefit is exactly the right call. An alias that resolves confidently to the wrong thing is worse than no alias at all, because the collaborator does not know they have been misdirected.

Section nine is rarer still. A register of terms that must not be forced through an alias is not something I have often seen written down, and it will prevent the worst failure this system could produce, which is quietly filing someone under something merely adjacent. Population Health is not Public Health, Kinesiology is not Exercise Science, and Wealth Management is neither Financial Planning nor Investment Management. Recording that explicitly means it stays true after both of us have forgotten why.

Section twelve is effectively the implementation specification, and I can build from it as written. Section eight likewise: the normalisation rules are implementable exactly as listed.

Three implementation notes, none of which requires anything from you.

Normalisation has to be applied to both sides, so the stored term and the typed term are reduced the same way before being compared. Otherwise Ageing finds Aging but Aging does not find Ageing, which is the sort of asymmetry nobody notices until a collaborator complains.

On the ambiguous abbreviations, you suggest PT might work as a contextual suggestion within a Field selector. I would do something slightly simpler. Rather than making suggestions depend on context, an ambiguous abbreviation will return all its plausible candidates, ranked below exact and true alias matches, each clearly labelled. The person sees Physical Therapy and anything else it might mean, and chooses. Same outcome, considerably less machinery, and nothing behaving differently depending on which screen you are on.

And your instinct to keep Discovery Terms out of this Register is right, and it is the same separation I described for Longevity. Classification aliases help a person describe themselves. Discovery terms help a person find others. Conflating them would mean somebody searching Healthy Aging accidentally classifying themselves as something.

Now the Credential Library, which is the important one

You were right to ask before developing it further, and the answer is reassuring: five of your six questions were settled when we discussed this at the start of September, and the sixth is new but small.

What each record holds. The acronym, its full meaning, the jurisdiction or country where that matters, and the credential type. Alongside those, the administrative fields the library needs for itself: whether it is approved or still pending, who proposed it, and when.

How a collaborator selects up to three. They type, and the library searches as they go, matching the full meaning as well as the acronym. Somebody who holds a Master of Public Health but does not think of themselves as an MPH can type public health and still find it. They select entries rather than typing text, and may display up to three after their name.

When they cannot find it. The request option sits on that same screen. They enter the acronym and its meaning, their profile saves, and they continue. The entry is attached to them and marked as awaiting Institute approval, visible to you and to them but not displayed publicly and not offered to anybody else. Nobody is ever held up waiting for a decision. The request form also shows near matches first, so nobody proposes Masters in Public Health when Master of Public Health already exists.

What you can do with a request. Approve it, decline it, or correct the wording before approving, which is the one that matters most. Because collaborators select an entry rather than typing text, standardising the wording corrects every profile using it at once.

Whether the credential has its own record rather than the acronym being the identifier. Yes, and this is the answer to the complexity you have run into. MD is Doctor of Medicine in most of the world and Managing Director in others. FRCP differs between London, Edinburgh, Glasgow and Canada. If the acronym were the identifier, those could not coexist. Because each credential is its own record with its own identity, the same letters can appear several times with different meanings and jurisdictions, and the meaning shown beside each one is how a person picks the right one. Your instinct to store the full meaning was what made this possible.

Notification after review. This is the genuinely new one. I had not specified it, and you are right that it is missing. When you approve a proposed credential it should simply appear on the collaborator's profile, and I would tell them so in a short message, since they will be wondering. If you decline, I would say nothing automatically, and leave whether to explain to your judgement, in the same spirit as the other decisions the Portal leaves with you.

That last piece is small enough that I am absorbing it. Nothing in your six questions turns this into a larger feature than the one already included, and the figure is unchanged.
