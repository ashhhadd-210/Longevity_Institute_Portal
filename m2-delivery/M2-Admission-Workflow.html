Milestone 2 — Admission Workflow & Product Direction · Waga C, Pronto Digital · 18 August 2026

This document does two things. First, it sets out the admission workflow for your comment before any of the review interface is built, incorporating the correction you made about review decisions. Second, it answers your Wish List by sorting every item into the three categories you asked for, and gives you my recommendation on the four questions where you asked for architectural advice.

1. Your correction, and the principle behind it

You are right, and the correction is more than a wording change. There is a real distinction underneath it that will keep the Institute's records clean for years, so it is worth stating plainly.

A review produces three possible actions, but only two of them are decisions.

ActionWhat it isWhat it does to the case
Approve A final judgement by the Institute Closes the review. Recorded permanently. Makes an invitation possible.
Decline A final judgement by the Institute Closes the review. Recorded permanently, with your reasoning.
Need More Information Not a judgement — a request Keeps the case open and awaiting a response. May happen more than once.

This matters because the permanent admission record should contain the Institute's judgements, not its correspondence. If "need more information" were stored as a decision, then a candidate you asked two questions of before approving would appear to have been decided upon three times. Instead, information requests are recorded as their own kind of event — visible in the history, fully audited, but never confused with a verdict.

And you are equally right that issuing an invitation is not a review decision. It is the step that follows approval. I should note that the system already reflects this: an invitation carries a reference to the approval that authorised it, which is precisely why the Institute will be able to answer, years from now, not merely that someone was invited but on whose approval and on what reasoning.

4. The four questions where you asked for advice

a. Three title / organisation combinations — and why I am glad you raised it now

Today the model holds one professional title and one organisation for each collaborator. What you have described — a professor who also holds a hospital appointment and a directorship — is not a bigger version of that. It is a different shape: a person has several positions, each with its own title, organisation and possibly its own dates.

Caught now, this is a small, additive change to a model that is not yet carrying real profiles. Caught during Milestone 3, it would have meant rebuilding the profile screens after they were built. Caught after launch, it would have meant migrating real people's data. This is exactly the kind of correction that is cheap today and expensive in October, and it is why I would rather have your Wish List early and long than late and tidy.

My recommendation: up to three positions, each with title, organisation and an optional "primary" marker so the directory knows which one to show in the collapsed view. Where someone is independent, the organisation is simply left empty and — per your formatting principle — nothing appears.

b. Photograph standards: what software can and cannot enforce

I want to be honest about the line here, because it affects how the standard should be written.

Software can enforce: that the file is a genuine photograph in an accepted format; a minimum resolution so it does not appear blurred; a maximum file size; that it is not animated; and a consistent rectangular crop, presented consistently across the Portal.

Software cannot reliably enforce: that it is head-and-shoulders, that no hat is worn, or that it was taken within five years. Attempting to detect those automatically would produce confident wrong answers and reject good photographs.

So my recommendation is: state the standard in plain language at the point of upload — where it will actually be read — and let your profile review step be the place it is applied. The two administrative steps you proposed in item 6 are precisely the right mechanism for this: the photograph is checked by a person, at review, before publication.

c. What belongs in the initial directory, and what should wait

My recommendation for the initial directory: browse, plus search by name, by area of expertise, and by country. Those three, and no more, for a reason: they are the only attributes that will be reliably and consistently populated for every collaborator from the first day. A filter that returns empty results because half the community has not filled in that field teaches collaborators that search does not work.

Organisation search should wait until the organisation records have been tidied centrally — see (d) — otherwise "Karolinska Institutet" and "Karolinska Institute" become two different places.

One clarification I would like from you: you list professional discipline/specialty and area of expertise as separate things. My strong recommendation is that these be one vocabulary with two levels — discipline, and specialties within it — rather than two independent lists. With two lists, the same idea gets recorded in both places differently and neither can be trusted. With one structured vocabulary, a collaborator can be found by the broad discipline or the narrow specialty, and the Institute keeps control of both.

As for richer filtering: I would let the community tell us. At roughly a hundred collaborators, browsing a well-presented directory is genuinely pleasant and filters add little. The moment the directory becomes uncomfortable to browse — in my experience somewhere past a hundred and fifty — is the moment to build filtering, and by then we will know from actual searches what people are looking for. Building it earlier means guessing.

d. Organisations: structured records now, a directory only if it earns its place

My recommendation is the middle option you suggested, and it is largely done already: organisations are structured records linked to profiles, not free text typed into each profile. Milestone 1 built them that way.

That gives the Institute three things immediately: consistent naming, the ability to ask "who here is at Kyoto University" later, and the option to give organisations their own pages in future without migrating anything.

What I would not build yet is a public organisation directory. Until an organisation record carries something worth reading — a description, a link, the nonprofit's purpose, the collaborators associated with it — a directory of organisations is a list of names, and a list of names invites the question "why is this here?". The structure is what matters now; the surface can wait until you know what it is for. If the nonprofit dimension turns out to be central to the Institute's work, that is exactly the evidence that would justify building it.

e. Communication: my recommendation for the first Beta is no messaging

You asked what I recommend rather than assuming a full messaging system, so let me answer directly: for the first Beta, I recommend no messaging at all.

Instead, the "I would like to collaborate" mechanism you described earlier: a collaborator viewing a profile can record an interest in working with that person, and that interest becomes a record the Institute can see.

Four reasons I prefer this for the Beta:

  • The people you are inviting already have email, and they are practised at using it. A private messaging system asks distinguished professionals to check somewhere new.
  • Messaging creates an expectation of responsiveness. An unanswered message inside a curated community reads as a slight; an unanswered email does not.
  • A private message is invisible to the Institute. An interest record is visible — and the Institute's actual value here is making the introduction, which is far more valuable to both parties than a message box.
  • Those records tell us, with evidence, who wanted to reach whom. If messaging is genuinely needed, the interest records will show it, and will show what kind.

If, during the Beta, the same pairs of people repeatedly express interest and the Institute finds itself relaying introductions by hand, that is the signal to build messaging — and we will know exactly what it needs to do.

f. Metrics: the one item where waiting is genuinely expensive

Of everything on your list, this is the item I would treat most urgently — not the dashboard, but what it will one day show.

Analytics cannot be backfilled. A dashboard built next year can only display history that was recorded this year. If the Portal records only current totals — how many collaborators exist today — then a year from now the Institute can never ask how long admission took, where candidates dropped out, or how quickly profiles were completed. If it records events as they happen, then any dashboard we build later can look backwards over the Institute's entire history.

The good news is that the mechanism is already in place: the permanent record built in Milestone 1 is an event stream. My recommendation is simply that we record these deliberately from the start, at no meaningful cost:

  • The admission funnel with timestamps — application or referral received, reviewed, information requested, decided, invitation issued, invitation delivered, invitation accepted, registration completed. From this, later, the Institute can see how long each stage takes and where candidates are lost.
  • Invitation delivery outcomes — already recorded, per message.
  • Profile completion progress — when a collaborator starts, how far they get, where they abandon. This is how you will find out which part of the profile is too much to ask.
  • First sign-in, and returns — whether admitted collaborators actually come back, which is the single most honest measure of whether the Portal is useful.
  • Directory searches, in aggregate — what people look for and how often they find nothing. This is what will tell us which filters to build, rather than guessing.
  • Expressions of collaboration interest — who is trying to connect, and about what.

Deliberately not recorded: anything that amounts to surveillance of individuals. The purpose is to understand the community, not to watch its members, and that distinction should be visible in what the Institute chooses to keep.

5. Scope assessment against the agreed Foundation Release

You asked me not to treat the Wish List as an expansion of Milestone 2, and I have not. But the proposal made a commitment I intend to keep: that additions would be identified and priced openly at the time, and never quietly absorbed by reducing testing, documentation or security. Several items on your list are genuine additions to what was contracted, so here is the honest accounting — offered now, before any of it is built, so that neither of us is surprised in October.

For reference, the agreed Foundation Release commits to: profiles with a single position and organisation; a directory with browse and pagination; search by name and area of expertise; an Institute-managed expertise vocabulary; and directory visibility control supporting the phased launch. The Brief itself notes that additional search capability and advanced filtering "may be introduced in later milestones".

These stay in the product backlog. Each is designed for as an extension point, so none of them will require rebuilding anything when their time comes:

  • The Events section
  • Member-to-member messaging, curated forums, live polls
  • Institute-wide broadcast communication (the sending foundation and a separate bulk channel are already provided for)

To be explicit about what will not happen: I will not quietly absorb the items in section (b) by spending less time on testing, documentation or security, and I will not quietly drop them either. They are listed here so that you can decide, with the full picture, what belongs in the Foundation Release and what waits. If you would prefer to defer all of them, the Foundation Release remains exactly what was agreed, and every one of them can still be added later without rework — which was the entire point of building the foundation the way we did.
